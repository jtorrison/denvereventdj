How Many Speakers Do You Need for a Wedding or Event?

It depends more on the space than the guest count. Plan sound for each area separately: the ceremony needs clear voices, cocktail hour and dinner need even background music, and the dance floor needs punch. Outdoor sites, long rooms and tents usually need more coverage than a square indoor room.

By Josh Torrison, Denver wedding and event DJ since 2011

You don't need to know any of this to book a DJ. A good one will ask about your space and size the setup for you. But if you're curious, or comparing quotes, here's how I think about it.

Think in areas, not headcount

A hundred guests in a compact ballroom is a very different job from a hundred guests spread across a lawn. Sound fades fast outdoors because there are no walls to bounce it back. Long rooms and tents get quiet at the far end. So instead of asking "how many guests," start with "where will people be, and for which part of the day?"

Ceremony sound

The ceremony is about voices. Every guest should hear your vows without straining. For most ceremonies, a pair of good powered speakers on stands, aimed at the seating, does the job. Bigger crowds or wide, shallow seating can need more.

Mics matter as much as speakers:

  • A lavalier (clip-on) mic for the officiant keeps their hands free.
  • A handheld wireless mic for vows and readings.
  • Ceremony music needs to start and stop on cue, so someone has to be watching.

Cocktail hour and dinner

If cocktail hour is in a different space from the ceremony, it needs its own sound, or time for the setup to move. Dinner wants even, low-level music so people can talk across the table. You don't need big bass at dinner. If something isn't needed, like subwoofers at a quiet dinner, leave it out.

The dance floor

This is where a subwoofer earns its spot. Dance music lives in the low end, and a floor without it feels thin. In a long room or tent, the goal is a dance floor that hits hard without blasting the tables in the back, which sometimes means placing speakers thoughtfully instead of just adding more.

If you have a band, the DJ can often run through the band's system for the DJ sets. That's what happened at Boulder Flower Farm: I played dinner, the band played their set, then I took over the dance floor through their system.

Outdoor sites and power

Outdoor ceremonies are some of my favorites, but they need planning:

  • Power. Mountain overlooks and backyard corners often have no outlet. I bring battery-powered speakers and wireless mics so the ceremony can happen anywhere. If a generator is the only option, remember it's loud, so place it as far from the guests as you can.
  • Wind. It's the enemy of microphones. A lavalier tucked out of the wind beats a handheld in a gust.
  • Distance. If the ceremony site is a walk from the reception, plan time to move gear, or set up two systems.
  • Weather. Colorado afternoons can turn fast. Know the indoor or tent backup, and make sure the sound plan works there too.

Questions to ask your venue

  • Where are the power outlets, and how far are they from the ceremony and dance floor?
  • Is there a sound limit or a music curfew?
  • Does the venue have its own sound system or a preferred AV company?
  • What's the rain or wind backup, and is there power there?
  • When can vendors load in, and how far is the walk from parking?
