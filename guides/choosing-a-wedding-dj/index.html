5 Things to Consider When Picking a Wedding DJ

The short version: know who will actually DJ your wedding, how they plan the music with you, whether they read the room or just play a set, how they handle the ceremony and the moments in between, and how well they work with your other vendors. Get those five right and the rest is easy to compare.

By Josh Torrison, Denver wedding and event DJ since 2011

In this guide

  2. How they plan the music with you

  4. The ceremony and the moments in between

  6. Questions to ask before you book

I've DJed more than 100 weddings and events since my first paid gig in 2011. Couples almost never ask me the questions that matter most. They ask about speakers and song libraries, which every working DJ has covered. The real differences are about people, planning and judgment. Here's what I'd look at if I were hiring a DJ for my own wedding.

1. Who actually shows up

Some DJ companies book you with the owner and send whoever is free that weekend. That isn't automatically bad, but you should know before you sign. The person you plan with should be the person behind the decks, or at least someone you've talked to.

Ask it straight: "Will you personally be our DJ?" If the answer is "one of our DJs," ask who, and ask to talk to them before the wedding.

For what it's worth, when you book me you get me. No roster, no surprise replacement.

2. How they plan the music with you

A good DJ wants to know your people, not just your songs. Who's coming? Is it a dance crowd or a dinner-and-drinks crowd? Are there songs that mean something to your family? Songs you never want to hear again?

Look for a real planning process: a call or Zoom after you book, a way to share playlists, a do-not-play list they treat as a hard rule, and check-ins as the date gets close. I set up shared Spotify playlists with my couples so they can add songs whenever they think of them. Some couples give me a direction and let me run with it. Others hand me the exact songs. Both work.

The red flag is a DJ whose whole process is "send me a list." If they never ask about the crowd, they're planning a set, not your wedding.

3. Whether they read the room

This is where DJs really differ. Some play a set they built in advance no matter what's happening on the floor. Others mix live and change direction the second a song isn't landing.

Weddings need the second kind. Your crowd might be grandparents, college friends and coworkers in the same room (here's how to plan music for all ages). Early in the night I play upbeat, familiar music that gets every generation dancing. If grandma gets up, she's hearing a song she knows. Later, when the crowd shifts, the music shifts with it.

Ask: "What do you do when the dance floor empties?" A good answer sounds like a plan. A bad answer sounds like "that doesn't happen." If you're weighing a playlist against a DJ, I wrote a whole guide on playlists vs. a live DJ.

4. The ceremony and the moments in between

The dance floor gets the attention, but the moments that go wrong are usually quieter: a vow mic that cuts out, a processional song that ends before the bride reaches the aisle, a toast nobody in the back can hear.

Ask whether the DJ handles ceremony sound, including wireless mics for the officiant and your vows, and what they do if the ceremony site has no power. Ask who runs announcements and keeps the timeline moving. That's the MC job, and I explain it in do you need an MC at your wedding. If your ceremony is outside, read how many speakers you need too.

Small details make a big difference here. One couple's walk-down song was too short for their aisle, so I made a custom edit that ran long enough. That's the kind of thing a good DJ catches during planning, not on the day.

5. How they work with your other vendors

Your DJ touches almost every vendor: the venue's sound rules and curfew, the planner's timeline, the photographer's big moments, the band's setup. One wedding I played this summer had more than 25 vendors, and we all worked off the same plan.

Ask how the DJ coordinates with your planner and venue, and whether they've worked alongside a band. At Jono and Sloane's wedding at Boulder Flower Farm, I played dinner, handed off to the band, then took the dance floor through their sound system until the venue's hard stop. Nobody noticed the handoffs, which is the point. More in having a band and a DJ at your wedding.

Questions to ask before you book

  • Will you personally DJ our wedding?
  • Can we see video or hear a mix from a real wedding you played?
  • How do you plan the music with us, and how many check-ins do we get?
  • Do you handle ceremony sound and wireless mics? What if there's no power?
  • Do you MC? How much talking do you do?
  • How do you handle requests and our do-not-play list?
  • Have you worked with a band, or at our venue?
  • What's your backup plan if a laptop or speaker fails?
  • Are you insured, and can you send our venue a certificate of insurance?
  • What exactly is included in your price?

On that last one: compare quotes on what's included, not just the number. My packages are on the pricing page, and if you have another quote, send it over and I'll walk you through the differences.

My take

Hire the person you trust to read your people. Gear can be rented. Judgment can't.
