How to Plan Wedding Music for a Crowd of All Ages

Your grandparents, your college friends, your parents' friends and a few kids, all on one dance floor. It's the hardest crowd in music and my favorite one. Here's how the night comes together so everybody gets a turn.

By Josh Torrison, Denver wedding and event DJ since 2011

In this guide

  1. Know who's coming
  2. Build the night in order
  3. Songs that bridge generations
  4. Planned moments for everyone
  5. Requests and the do-not-play list
  6. Reading the room

A wedding isn't a club night. Nobody chose this crowd for its taste in music. That's what makes it fun: when the right song hits, you get three generations on the floor at once.

Know who's coming

Before the wedding, I want a rough picture of the room. Tell your DJ:

  • The rough mix of ages, and which group is most likely to dance
  • Whether kids will be there, and until when (it changes the lyrics plan, see clean vs. explicit)
  • Family traditions or cultural dances you want to include
  • A few songs your parents or grandparents love
  • The music your friends will lose it over late at night

Build the night in order

The order matters more than any single song. Here's how I usually build it:

  1. Open with songs everyone knows. Upbeat, familiar, easy to dance to. This is when grandparents and kids get on the floor, and the early crowd brings everyone else with them.
  2. Move through the eras. Motown into the 80s into the 2000s into today. Each switch brings a new group to the floor without losing the last one.
  3. Build energy as the crowd shifts. As the older guests head home, the floor gets younger, and the music follows.
  4. Close with something everyone can sing. The last song should bring everybody back.

Songs that bridge generations

Some songs work for almost everyone: the classics people grew up hearing at every wedding, songs that came back around for a new generation, and big singalongs. College house parties taught me the power of a familiar song. When the room is mixed, a song everyone recognizes in the first few seconds beats a perfect deep cut every time.

What doesn't bridge: long intros, slow builds and anything only one table knows. Save those for later, when the floor has found its groove.

Planned moments for everyone

  • Parent dances. A moment for your parents' generation, and a chance to pull them onto the floor right after.
  • Cultural and family traditions. At Jono and Sloane's wedding, guests lifted the couple into the air during the hora. Moments like that bring every age together.
  • An anniversary dance or group dance. A fun way to celebrate the long marriages in the room.

Requests and the do-not-play list

Requests are how a mixed crowd tells you what it wants. I take them and fit them in where they'll land, not the second they're asked. And your do-not-play list is a hard rule, whoever asks. Keep both in one place with the wedding music planner.

Reading the room

School dances taught me to change direction fast when a track isn't landing. At a wedding, that means watching who's on the floor, who's at the edge waiting for their song, and who just sat down. That's the difference between a playlist and a live DJ: a playlist plays the next song no matter what.

My take

Start where everyone can join, then take turns. The best nights feel like each generation got its moment.

Quick answers

How do you keep both grandparents and college friends dancing?

Order and range. Songs every generation knows open the floor, then the night builds and moves between eras so everyone gets a turn. Later, when the crowd shifts younger, the music shifts with it.

Should we give our DJ a list of songs for older guests?

A few is great, especially songs that mean something to your parents or grandparents. You don't need a full list. A good DJ reads who's on the floor.

What if most of our guests are one age group?

Then the music leans that way, with a few moments for everyone else, like a parent dance, a classic everyone knows or a family tradition.

How do we include a cultural dance or tradition?

Tell your DJ early what it is, when it happens and which song or version you want. Planned moments like a hora land best when everyone knows the cue.
