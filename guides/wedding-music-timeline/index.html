Wedding Music, Moment by Moment

A wedding isn't one long playlist. Guest arrival, the ceremony, cocktail hour, dinner, the big moments and the dance floor each need a different feel. Here's how I plan each part with my couples, and how much of it you actually need to decide yourself.

By Josh Torrison, Denver wedding and event DJ since 2011

The one rule: don't try to do too much

Couples sometimes come to me with a spreadsheet of 300 songs. You don't need that. Pick the songs that matter for the key moments, give me a few must-plays and a do-not-play list, and let me handle the rest. Your job that day is to enjoy your evening, not to manage a playlist.

Guest arrival and the ceremony

Guest arrival (about 20 to 30 minutes)

Keep it relaxed and warm. Acoustic covers, soft classics or instrumental versions of songs you love. This is a nice place for personal favorites that don't fit the dance floor.

Processional

Usually one song for the wedding party and a separate song for the bride or the couple. Timing matters more than anything here. Walk the aisle at your venue if you can, or tell me how long it is, so the song fits. If your favorite song is too short, I can make a custom edit so it lasts. I did exactly that for Owen Bailey's walk-down song at Ruedi Reservoir.

The ceremony itself

Most of the ceremony is spoken, so this is a sound job more than a music job. Your officiant needs a clear mic, your vows need a mic, and any readers need one too. If there's a unity ceremony or a signing, a soft song underneath fills the space.

Recessional

Go big and joyful. You just got married. This is the song that tells everyone the party is starting.

Cocktail hour and dinner

Cocktail hour can be upbeat, but people are talking, so think energy without volume. If cocktail hour is in a different spot than the ceremony, plan for sound there too.

Dinner is background music. Keep it at a level where people can talk across the table. This is a great time for songs from your story: the song from your first road trip, your parents' favorites, music from where you met.

Entrance, first dance, toasts and cake

Grand entrance

High energy and short. Pick a song that hits right away, not one with a long intro. I'll line up the wedding party, cue the photographer and introduce everyone.

First dance

Pick the song that means something to you, then decide on length. Three minutes feels long when everyone is watching, so a lot of couples cut it down. Another option: a custom edit that starts slow and intimate, then drops into a full dance mix so the floor opens up right after. I made one for Luca and McKensey at Moss Denver, and you can hear it on the homepage.

Parent dances

Same idea as the first dance. If you want, I can blend two songs or shorten one so it ends on a high note. At a castle wedding in Tuscany, I made a custom edit for the father-daughter dance.

Toasts

No music under toasts, just a good mic and someone keeping things moving. Decide the order ahead of time and give your speakers a rough time limit. It's a kindness to everyone.

Cake cutting

A short, fun song. Something sweet or a little cheesy works great here.

The dance floor

Open dancing should build. I start with familiar, upbeat songs that work for every generation, so grandparents and kids get on the floor early. Then the energy climbs as the night goes on. When the late-night crowd is ready, the music can go somewhere different.

What helps me most from you:

  • Must-plays. A handful of songs, not fifty. These are your guaranteed moments.
  • Do-not-plays. Treated as a hard rule.
  • A direction. "Throwbacks and hip-hop," "house music late," "lots of country." Tell me the vibe and trust me with the rest.
  • Request rules. Anyone can request, just the wedding party, or nobody. Your call.
  • Lyrics. Clean all night, or explicit after a certain point? See clean vs. explicit.

Last dance and send-off

End on a song everyone can sing. If you're doing a sparkler exit or a send-off, pick something celebratory and give me a heads-up so the timing works with your photographer.
