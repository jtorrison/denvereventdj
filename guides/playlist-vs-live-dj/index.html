Wedding Playlist vs. a Live DJ: What's the Difference?

A playlist plays songs in order. A live DJ watches the room and decides what comes next. A playlist is fine for cocktail hour or a small dinner. For a dance floor, ceremony cues, announcements and a crowd of all ages, you want a person running the music.

By Josh Torrison, Denver wedding and event DJ since 2011

In this guide

  5. If you go the playlist route

I get this question a lot, and I'll be honest about it even though I'm a DJ. A playlist isn't always the wrong call. It's just a different tool, and it's good at different things.

What a preprogrammed mix actually is

"Preprogrammed" usually means one of two things: a Spotify or Apple Music playlist someone hits play on, or a mix a DJ recorded in advance and hands you as one long file. Either way, the order is locked before anyone walks into the room.

Where a playlist works fine

  • Background music. Cocktail hour, dinner and guest arrival don't need live mixing. They need the right songs at the right volume.
  • Small gatherings with no dancing planned. A rehearsal dinner or a backyard brunch is perfect playlist territory.
  • Some ceremony moments. Even I use downloaded playlists for parts of a ceremony. The difference is that someone who knows the cues is pressing play.

Where a playlist falls apart

It can't read the room

When a song empties the dance floor, a playlist keeps playing it for three more minutes. A DJ is already blending into the next one. Over a whole night, those small saves are the difference between a packed floor and people drifting to the bar.

The big moments run on cues, not a clock

Your processional starts when the bride is at the end of the aisle, not at 4:07. The grand entrance starts when the wedding party is lined up. Someone has to watch for those moments and press play. With a playlist, that's usually a friend or cousin who would rather be enjoying your wedding.

Nobody is on the mic

Someone needs to welcome your entrance, call tables to dinner, gather people for toasts and the first dance, and keep the night moving. A playlist can't do that. Here's what a wedding MC actually does.

Requests and surprises

Your uncle wants one specific song. A toast runs long. The rain moves everything inside. A person adjusts. A playlist doesn't.

Sound that fits the room

A phone and a party speaker can sound great in a living room. A tent with 120 guests, a long hall or an outdoor ceremony is a different job. You need enough sound for the space, mics that don't feed back, and someone watching levels so dinner stays conversational and the dance floor actually hits.

The middle ground

You don't have to choose all or nothing. Plenty of couples run a playlist for a cocktail hour in another space and have a DJ for dinner and the dance floor. Others book a shorter, reception-only package. You can see the options on the pricing page.

If you go the playlist route

If a playlist is the right call for your event, set yourself up for it:

  • Download everything for offline play. Venue wifi and cell service fail all the time, especially in the mountains.
  • Use a premium account so ads never play.
  • Check that the clean versions are the ones in the playlist if kids or grandparents will be there. More on that in clean vs. explicit wedding music.
  • Turn on a short crossfade so there's no dead air between songs, and turn off autoplay so a random song doesn't start when the list ends.
  • Make separate playlists for each part of the night, and assign one sober, reliable person to run them.
  • Test the speaker in the actual space, at the actual volume, before guests arrive.

My take

Use a playlist where the music is background. Hire a person where the music has a job to do.
